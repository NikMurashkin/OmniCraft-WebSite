Calculation Diagnosis: Assessing the Financial Health of Your 3D Print Job ​

The built-in calculator categorizes any 3D printing estimate into three clear profitability zones:

1. Profit Illusion (Print Job is Unprofitable) ​

The selling price barely covers the raw filament or resin weight reported by your slicer, failing to compensate for support structures, print failures, brass inserts, IPA wash chemistry, and manual cleanup time.

  • What is happening: the print farm owner is essentially selling plastic off their shelf at cost while subsidizing nozzle wear, LCD screen degradation, and post-processing hours out of personal pocket.
  • Actionable step: stop quoting jobs by simply "multiplying slicer grams by $0.05", enable a 10–20% waste buffer for supports and failures, and bill for hands-on operator labor.

2. Hidden Risk (Fragile Profit Margin) ​

The estimate is technically in the black, but your net studio margin is under 15%.

  • What is happening: a single layer shift or bed detachment at hour 7, a clogged hardened nozzle on carbon-fiber filament, or a punctured FEP film in your resin vat will instantly wipe out all profit on the order.
  • Actionable step: factor in hourly 3D printer depreciation and extruder/LCD consumables, and increase your failure buffer when printing high-warp engineering polymers (ABS, ASA, PA-CF).

3. Healthy 3D Print Order (Genuine Profit) ​

The product pricing accounts for all true production factors and provides long-term financial stability for the print farm.

  • What is happening: the price fully covers filament or resin including supports and purge waste, brass heat-set inserts, IPA alcohol, protective packaging, and printer wear, while fairly compensating hands-on bench hours at your target rate.
  • Outcome for the farm: the workshop retains 15–20%+ clean profit margin to purchase replacement components, upgrade machinery, and maintain healthy working reserves.

Anatomy of Direct Materials: Polymers, Hardware & Post-Processing Chemistry ​

A common mistake in generic spreadsheets is lumping primary polymers, brass inserts, and isopropyl alcohol into a single list. In reality, each category has its own unit of measure and physical waste profile:

1. FDM Filaments and SLA / DLP Photopolymer Resins ​

Block 1 of the calculator tracks your primary polymer in kilograms (standard 1 kg spools or bottles), grams, or milliliters:

  • Standard & aesthetic FDM filaments (PLA, Silk PLA, PETG): cost-effective polymers for enclosures, prototypes, and decor ($15–$30/kg), which still require accounting for support structures and multicolor purge waste.
  • Engineering, flexible & composite filaments (ABS, ASA, TPU, Nylon, PA-CF, PETG-CF): functional materials costing $40–$150/kg. Require active filament drying, enclosed heated chambers, and a higher margin for thermal warping.
  • Photopolymer resins (Standard, ABS-Like, Tough, Flexible, Dental, Castable): liquid resins for SLA/MSLA printing. Beyond net model volume, 15–25% of resin is consumed by dense support rafts and uncured resin clinging to part surfaces washed off in IPA.

2. Fasteners, Heat-Set Inserts & Hardware ​

Functional 3D prints rarely ship as bare plastic. Block 2 tracks piece-count hardware:

  • Mechanical fasteners: knurled brass heat-set inserts (M2, M2.5, M3, M4, M5), socket head cap screws (DIN 912), captive hex nuts, ball bearings (608ZZ), and steel dowel pins.
  • Maker & electronic hardware: neodymium magnets (glued into recesses or embedded mid-print), keychain split rings, addressable LED strips, USB Type-C breakout boards, and switches.

3. Print Chemistry & Post-Processing Consumables ​

Block 3 captures shop supplies that aren't bought for a single job but are consumed across every batch:

  • FDM supplies: bed adhesion glue stick or 3D lacquer spray, hobby knife blades (for deburring brims), cyanoacrylate superglue and activator, sanding sponges, filler primer, and acrylic paint.
  • SLA resin supplies: 99.7% isopropyl alcohol (IPA) for wash stations, disposable nitrile gloves, lint-free paper towels, and mesh paint filters for straining resin back into bottles.

Three Financial Pitfalls of Commercial 3D Printing ​

Most beginner 3D print shops quietly lose money because they price jobs using a rule of thumb from hobbyist forums: "take the weight from the slicer and multiply by filament price per gram". For example, on a 120 g PETG instrument enclosure, the slicer shows just $2.80 in net plastic, whereas the true unit cost—once you add 25% support waste, 6 brass M3 inserts, 3.5 hours of printer wear, and 35 minutes of bench cleanup—exceeds $14.50. Here are the three financial traps that drain print farm profits.

1. Trusting Net Part Weight Without Supports, Purge Waste, and Print Failures ​

If a custom enclosure weighs 120 grams, your spool will never lose exactly 120 grams. Real-world additive manufacturing always incurs process waste:

  • Supports, brims, and rafts: complex overhangs and bridging geometries add 10–35% in sacrificial plastic or resin.
  • Prime towers and multicolor purge chutes (AMS / MMU "poop"): when printing 2–4 colors on automated filament changers, purged waste often weighs more than the finished model itself.
  • Statistical print failure risk: bed detachment at hour 4, layer shifts, heat-creep extruder clogs, or delamination in `ABS` require a complete reprint at your expense.
  • Unusable spool ends: the last 15–30 grams on a 1 kg spool are rarely enough for a production run and sit idle on shelves.
Watch for Hidden Waste: Why Slicer Weight Needs a Safety Buffer

Even if you enter the total job weight from your slicer (including supports and brim), always enable the waste toggle at 8–15% for standard PLA/PETG parts and 15–25% for high-warp engineering composites, multicolor prints, and SLA photopolymer resins.

2. The "Printer Works by Itself" Illusion (Unpaid Operator Labor) ​

Beginners often ignore their own time: "I just clicked print and the machine hummed overnight". Yet in commercial 3D printing, the operator performs substantial engineering and bench work:

  • CAD inspection and slicing: repairing non-manifold STL/STEP meshes, orienting parts along load vectors, painting custom supports, and tuning slicer profiles.
  • Machine setup and turnaround: loading spools into filament dryers, washing and degreasing PEI plates, applying bed adhesive, inspecting the first layer, and harvesting parts.
  • Post-processing and assembly: snapping off supports, deburring brims with a blade, heat-gun string removal, IPA washing and UV curing for resins, and pressing brass inserts with a soldering iron.
The Print Farm Law: Separate Operator Time from Machine Print Time

A bracket may take 9 hours to print on the machine, yet require only 0.5 hours (30 minutes) of active operator labor for setup, support removal, and heat-set insert installation. Enter your hands-on specialist time in the "Operator Time (h)" field, and enter the slicer print duration in the dedicated "Printer Runtime (h)" field inside the depreciation block.

3. Ignoring Nozzle, PEI Plate, FEP Film, and LCD Screen Wear ​

A 3D printer is a thermal and optical machine with consumables that wear out every hour. If you don't allocate equipment wear into every quote, replacing a hotend assembly or LCD screen will wipe out a month of profit:

Practical Accounting: What 1 Hour of 3D Printer Runtime Actually Costs

On an FDM printer, nozzles wear out regularly (carbon-fiber PA-CF ruins a brass nozzle in a single spool and requires $20–$45 hardened nozzles), along with flexible PEI build plates ($25), heaters, fans, and belts. On an SLA resin printer, a $100–$180 monochrome LCD screen lasts 1,500–2,000 hours, while FEP/ACF release films require replacement every 20–40 vats. Combined with printer and filament dryer electricity (0.15–0.35 kWh), direct machine-hour cost ranges from $0.50 to $1.50/hour.

The "Lightbulb Over the Workbench" Principle: Allocating Fixed Print Farm Overhead

Your hourly wear rate (Awear) only covers direct machine depreciation while stepper motors spin and heaters run. Every studio also carries standing overhead that follows the "lightbulb over the workbench" principle: climate-controlled studio rent, active exhaust ventilation for styrene and resin fumes, continuous filament dryer operation, and shop internet for camera monitoring. These bills tick away every day regardless of printer utilization—divide your monthly fixed shop expenses by your average monthly order count and enter the result in "Fixed Overhead per Order (Ofixed)".

Frequently Asked Questions ​

Why shouldn't I price 3D prints strictly per gram of filament?

Pricing strictly "per gram" ignores geometry complexity, machine occupation time, and manual cleanup. For example, an intricate 10-gram miniature gear printed at 0.08 mm layer height with dense tree supports can tie up a printer for 3 hours and require 25 minutes of careful scalpel cleanup, whereas a solid 100-gram spacer printed with a 0.6 mm nozzle finishes in 1 hour and pops off the bed in 10 seconds.

True Quote Model
Part Price=Polymer (g)+Inserts & Chemistry+Operator Labor (h)+Printer Wear

Separating material weight, hands-on labor, and printer depreciation protects your studio from losing money on small, support-heavy jobs.

How do I calculate hourly 3D printer depreciation?

Divide your 3D printer purchase price by its expected service lifespan before major overhaul (typically 3,000–4,000 print hours) and add hourly consumable wear and electricity.

FDM Depreciation
Ahour=$1,000 (printer)3,500 h (lifespan)+$0.25 (nozzles, PEI, power)≈$0.54/print hour

For SLA resin printers, always include monochrome LCD screen replacement (for example, a $120 screen lasting 2,000 hours adds $0.06 per exposure hour on top of FEP film wear).

How do I account for multicolor filament purge waste and SLA resin supports?

Modern slicers display the total filament consumed by the job (net model + supports + prime tower and multicolor purge waste). Enter that total slicer weight in the "Quantity" column, and keep the waste toggle enabled at 8–15% as your statistical safety buffer against mid-print failures and unusable spool ends.

Polymer Allocation
Gross Weight=(Model + Supports + Purge)×(1 + Failure Risk %)

For SLA photopolymer printing, add 10–15% on top of your slicer's supported volume: uncured liquid resin clings to the surface of parts and rafts and is washed away in the IPA bath.

How should a 3D printing studio account for sales tax: deducted from earnings or included in part price?

Tax is assessed on the gross invoice amount for printing and finishing. Deducting tax from operator labor pay or machine depreciation reserves is a fundamental business mistake.

3D Print Tax Formula
Stotal=Sprint+(Ppart×Tax Rate %100)

On an order priced at $50, a 6% revenue tax is $3.00. This amount is added to production cost ($28.50) to create total landed cost ($31.50). The client pays the tax in their bill, safeguarding your hourly technician rate and equipment reserve.
